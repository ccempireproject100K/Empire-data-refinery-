# Food, beverage and agriculture — 20/20 sector report

## Bottom line

**Sector assessment: financially attractive, but uneven.** Food, beverage and agriculture combine essential, recurring demand with large value-added processing, branded products, distribution, restaurants, farm inputs, and agricultural technology. The most attractive economics are generally in differentiated brands, scaled processing/ingredients, distribution networks, farm-input and equipment technology, and businesses with recurring or service-like revenue. Commodity production and trading can be very large but are more cyclical and exposed to weather, prices, geopolitics, and working-capital swings.

This is a **sector map, not a ranking**. Company names below are candidates for further diligence, selected for scale, strategic relevance, or public evidence of operating capability; they are not claimed to be the “best” companies.

## Why it can be lucrative in the 2020s

1. **Essential and recurring consumption.** USDA estimates that agriculture, food, and related industries contributed approximately **$1.537 trillion, or 5.5% of U.S. GDP, in 2023**; farms alone contributed $222.3 billion. The related-industry definition includes food and beverage manufacturing, food and beverage stores, food services, and eating/drinking places. U.S. households allocated **12.9% of budgets to food in 2024**, making food a large, recurring spend category even when consumers trade down. ([USDA ERS, Ag and Food Sectors and the Economy](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/ag-and-food-statistics-charting-the-essentials/ag-and-food-sectors-and-the-economy))

2. **Global volume and mix growth.** The OECD–FAO Agricultural Outlook projects global food consumption to grow **1.2% annually** over 2024–2033 and total use of agricultural and fisheries products **1.0% annually**. India and Southeast Asia are expected to account for **31% of global consumption growth by 2033**, driven by urbanization and rising affluence. This supports long-run volume, premiumization, protein, convenience, cold-chain, and processing demand. ()

3. **Value is created downstream of the farm.** USDA’s Food Dollar framework separates the farm share from the marketing share, including processing, transport, wholesale, retail, and foodservice costs. The framework provides detailed data by product and outlet and makes it possible to analyze where supply-chain value accrues rather than assuming that farm-gate economics represent the whole sector. ([USDA ERS Food Dollar](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/food-dollar)) **Judgment:** this makes branded manufacturing, ingredients, logistics, retail execution, and foodservice particularly relevant profit pools, although a high marketing share is not the same as a high profit margin.

4. **Pricing, brands, and mix can defend margins.** PepsiCo’s 2024 annual report shows **$91.854 billion of net revenue**, **$12.887 billion of reported operating profit**, and a **14.0% operating margin**, up from 13.1% in 2023. Management attributed operating-profit growth primarily to net pricing and productivity savings, partly offset by cost increases and lower organic volume. The report also shows a diversified food/beverage mix (58% food, 42% beverage). () This is measured evidence for one scaled branded company, not proof that the entire sector has pricing power.

5. **Productivity and automation address structural constraints.** Deere describes labor shortages, volatile input costs, tighter weather windows, and the need to do more with less as customer problems. Its precision-agriculture stack includes connectivity, telematics, sensors, automation, software, and analytics. In fiscal 2024, Deere’s Production and Precision Agriculture segment generated **$20.834 billion of net sales**, **$4.514 billion of operating profit**, and a **21.7% operating margin**, although sales and profit fell year over year as commodity prices, interest rates, inventories, and volumes weakened. () The opportunity is attractive, but the results also demonstrate cyclicality.

6. **Scale, processing complexity, and global trade create barriers.** OECD–FAO estimates that about **20% of calories cross borders** before consumption and expects trade volumes to continue growing. Large origination, processing, storage, distribution, and compliance networks can lower unit costs and improve reliability. ADM’s 2024 results illustrate scale: **$85.530 billion of revenue**, **$4.209 billion of total segment operating profit**, and **$2.780 billion of operating cash flow**. However, segment operating profit fell 28% year over year, showing that scale does not eliminate commodity-cycle risk. ([ADM FY2024 results](https://investors.adm.com/news/news-details/2025/ADM-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2024-Results-Provides-2025-Guidance/))

## Most relevant industries

- **Branded packaged food and snacks:** repeat purchases, brand equity, distribution, pricing/mix, and product innovation. Examples: snacks, breakfast, ambient meals, nutrition, and better-for-you products.
- **Non-alcoholic beverages:** concentrated brands, bottling/distribution systems, convenience, and premium functional beverages.
- **Food ingredients and processing:** sweeteners, starches, oils, flavors, proteins, specialty ingredients, and contract manufacturing. These sit between farms and consumer brands and can benefit from scale and customer qualification.
- **Grain, oilseed, and commodity merchandising/processing:** origination, storage, crushing, milling, trading, biofuels, and export logistics. Large and strategic, but exposed to crop cycles, basis/margin swings, trade policy, and geopolitical risk.
- **Animal protein, dairy, and plant protein:** essential protein demand plus processing, cold chain, branded and foodservice channels; margins are sensitive to feed, herd/flock cycles, disease, and regulation.
- **Agricultural inputs:** seeds, crop protection, fertilizer, biologicals, and farm services. Demand is tied to yields and planted area; innovation and switching costs can support economics, but regulation and commodity cycles matter.
- **Agricultural machinery, precision agriculture, and farm software:** equipment replacement plus higher-value telematics, autonomy, and input-optimization tools. The category can earn attractive margins when technology improves customer ROI, but equipment sales are cyclical.
- **Grocery, food distribution, restaurants, and food delivery:** large recurring transaction pools and valuable customer data. Economics vary widely: grocery is volume/efficiency driven; restaurants depend on unit economics, labor, real estate, and brand; delivery has convenience value but weaker customer satisfaction and high platform costs.
- **Food safety, testing, traceability, and cold-chain logistics:** compliance and risk reduction create willingness to pay; growth is linked to regulation, global supply-chain complexity, and retailer/consumer expectations.

## Customer-quality and satisfaction signals

- **Restaurant experience:** ACSI’s 2024 Restaurant and Food Delivery Study reports a **79/100** overall score for fast-food restaurants and **84/100** for full-service restaurants. For fast food, order accuracy scored 86, mobile-app quality 86, mobile reliability 85, and food quality 84. For full service, food quality and freshness scored 89, cleanliness 89, and order accuracy 92. These are direct customer-experience signals, not profitability measures. ()
- **Restaurant-chain signal:** In the same ACSI study, Chick-fil-A scored 83 among measured fast-food chains, KFC 81, and a four-way group including Culver’s, Panera, Arby’s, and Starbucks scored 80. ACSI states Chick-fil-A led individual fast-food chains for the tenth consecutive year. This is a measured satisfaction result, not a universal quality or investment ranking.
- **Delivery quality gap:** ACSI’s first food-delivery benchmark scored the category at **73**, below full-service and fast-food restaurants; Uber Eats scored 74, DoorDash 73, and Grubhub 71. The study reports that convenience-driven users were more satisfied than users ordering out of necessity, who were more frustrated with pricing. This is a useful warning that convenience demand can coexist with weak perceived value.
- **Grocery/customer metrics:** ACSI maintains a supermarket industry benchmark and describes its scores as customer-satisfaction measures. Use the current  and underlying study releases for comparable retailer data; do not combine different study years without checking methodology.
- **Operational quality signals to track:** repeat purchase and retention, net promoter/customer-satisfaction scores, complaint and recall rates, order accuracy, on-time/in-full delivery, app reliability, product availability, private-label penetration, price/value perception, and safety/quality certifications. These are recommended diligence metrics; the list itself is judgment, not a claim that every company discloses them consistently.

## Caveats

- **No cross-sector ranking is implied.** Packaged food, restaurants, commodity merchandising, farm inputs, and machinery have different capital intensity, cycle timing, and accounting.
- **Profit pools are not the same as value-added shares.** USDA’s marketing share includes costs, wages, transport, and other inputs; it should not be read as profit.
- **Commodity exposure is cyclical.** ADM and Deere’s 2024 results show that large scale can coexist with sharp year-over-year profit declines.
- **Consumer demand is not uniformly defensive.** Households may keep buying food while trading down, switching channels, reducing restaurant frequency, or choosing private label.
- **Climate, disease, water, labor, regulation, food safety, trade restrictions, and geopolitical shocks can change earnings quickly.** OECD–FAO expects productivity-led growth but also projects agricultural direct GHG emissions to rise 5% over the outlook period.
- **Customer satisfaction is directional.** ACSI is survey-based and useful for comparisons within its methodology, but it does not measure margins, retention economics, safety performance, or long-term brand value. Study years and samples should be kept consistent.
- **Company metrics are not always comparable.** ADM’s segment operating profit and PepsiCo’s core operating profit are non-GAAP or company-defined measures in parts of the cited disclosures; reported GAAP figures should be used for cross-company comparison.
