# 20/20 Data Refinery — Sector 03: Financial services and fintech

## Bottom line

**Financial services and fintech is financially lucrative in the 2020s because it monetizes very large, recurring transaction and balance-sheet flows.** Banks earn net interest income, fees, payments revenue, asset-management fees, and investment-banking revenue. Payments networks and processors earn small fees on enormous volumes with strong operating leverage. Fintechs can add software, data, distribution, and embedded-finance fees on top of those rails. The sector also benefits from continued digitization, declining cash use, instant-payment adoption, and still-unmet demand for credit and financial access.

This is a **sector-level judgment, not a measured ranking of sectors**. Profitability is uneven: regulated balance-sheet businesses face credit losses, funding costs, capital requirements, fraud, conduct risk, and rate-cycle volatility; many venture-backed fintechs remain unprofitable. The strongest evidence supports attractive economics for scaled banks, payment networks/processors, and selected profitable fintech platforms—not for every fintech business model.

## Why it can be profitable in the 2020s

### 1. Huge and growing payment revenue pool

McKinsey estimates that global payments handled **3.4 trillion transactions** and **$1.8 quadrillion of value** in 2023, generating a **$2.4 trillion revenue pool**. It estimates 7% annual revenue growth from 2018–2023 and projects roughly 5% annual growth through 2028, adding about $700 billion to reach $3.1 trillion. Payments would represent about 35% of the total banking revenue pool. These are estimates from a reputable industry study, not official national accounts, and should not be treated as a precise market-size fact. Source: [McKinsey, “Global payments in 2024”](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/financial-services/our-insights/global-payments-in-2024-simpler-interfaces-complex-reality).

The structural opportunity is digitization: McKinsey says global cash usage is at 80% of 2019 levels and still declining, while $26 trillion of payments remained in cash. It also identifies instant payments, digital public infrastructure, platform commerce, and integrated SME software as growth vectors. More than 50% of US SME spending went through vertical-specific software solutions in 2023 in McKinsey’s survey, supporting the case for embedded payments and software-plus-finance products. These observations are directional and geography-specific where stated.

### 2. Scale economics and recurring customer relationships

The economics are visible in large-company disclosures. **JPMorgan Chase** reported 2023 total net revenue of **$158.1 billion**, net income of **$49.6 billion**, and 21% return on tangible common equity. Its Commercial Banking segment reported record 2023 revenue of $15.5 billion, net income of $6.1 billion, and payments revenue of $8.3 billion. Source: [JPMorgan 2023 Annual Report](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/content/dam/jpmc/jpmorgan-chase-and-co/investor-relations/documents/annualreport-2023.pdf).

**Bank of America** reported 2024 revenue of **$101.9 billion** and net income of **$27.1 billion**. It described the revenue mix as 55% net interest income and 45% fees, demonstrating why diversified banks can monetize both lending/funding spreads and non-interest businesses. Its 2024 return on average tangible common shareholders’ equity was 12.92%. Source: [Bank of America 2024 Annual Report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/70858/000007085825000155/bofaannualreport2024.pdf).

These figures demonstrate realized profitability at two scaled incumbents; they do **not** prove that all banks or fintechs earn similar returns. They also include unusually favorable or unfavorable rate, credit, and accounting conditions depending on the year.

### 3. Digital adoption expands addressable customers and lowers distribution friction

The World Bank’s **Global Findex 2021** describes technology and pandemic-accelerated digital adoption as transforming access to finance, with digital payments serving as a gateway to savings, credit, and other services. Source: [World Bank, Global Findex Database 2021](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099818107072234182).

The Federal Reserve’s 2024 household survey shows a large, monetizable US customer base: 81% of adults had a credit card, 15% used buy-now-pay-later in the prior 12 months (up from 10% in 2021), and 6% were unbanked. At the same time, only 34% applied for credit in 2024, and one-third of applicants were denied or received less credit than requested. This indicates both an established market and remaining inclusion/underwriting opportunity. Source: [Federal Reserve, Economic Well-Being of US Households in 2024 — Banking and Credit](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2025-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2024-banking-and-credit.htm).

### 4. Payment networks can combine high volume with asset-light economics

Visa’s public annual-report site reports for fiscal 2025 **$40.0 billion net revenue**, **257.5 billion processed transactions**, and **$14.2 trillion payments volume**. This is a company-reported scale signal, not a claim that Visa’s margins or growth represent the whole sector. Source: [Visa Fiscal 2025 Annual Report site](https://annualreport.visa.com/).

The general mechanism is attractive: once a trusted network and authorization/fraud infrastructure are established, incremental transactions can carry relatively low marginal cost. However, interchange regulation, network competition, fraud, cyber risk, pricing pressure, and merchant routing can compress returns.

## Most relevant industries and subsectors

1. **Depository banking and consumer finance** — deposits, mortgages, cards, personal loans, SME lending, overdrafts, and payments. Core earnings are net interest income plus fees, with credit and funding risk.
2. **Commercial and transaction banking** — treasury management, cash management, working-capital finance, trade finance, and corporate payments. Client relationships can be sticky and cross-sellable.
3. **Payments networks and processors** — card networks, merchant acquiring, payment facilitation, cross-border payments, gateways, and fraud/authorization services.
4. **Digital banking and neobanks** — mobile-first deposits, cards, payments, lending, and subscription/interchange economics; profitability varies greatly by customer acquisition cost and funding model.
5. **Fintech infrastructure and embedded finance** — APIs, banking-as-a-service, ledger/core systems, identity, compliance, fraud, payroll, and payments embedded in vertical software and marketplaces.
6. **Wealth and asset management** — brokerage, advisory, private banking, ETFs, mutual funds, and alternatives; recurring fee income but sensitive to market levels and flows.
7. **Insurance technology and digital insurance** — distribution, underwriting, claims, and risk analytics; economics depend on loss ratios and reinsurance, not just software growth.
8. **Capital markets and investment banking** — underwriting, advisory, market making, securities services, and data; cyclical but highly profitable in active issuance and trading periods.
9. **Alternative lending, BNPL, and consumer-credit platforms** — potentially attractive yield and fee pools, but with materially higher credit, regulatory, and funding risk.
10. **Digital assets and stablecoin/payment infrastructure** — potentially useful for settlement and cross-border payments, but regulatory, custody, market, and adoption risks remain high.

Official data infrastructure for comparing these subsectors includes the [BIS statistics portal](https://data.bis.org/), which provides international banking, credit, liquidity, and payment statistics compiled with central banks and national authorities, and the [Federal Reserve Financial Accounts (Z.1)](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/).

## Candidate leading companies

The list below is a **candidate set for diligence, not a ranking**. Inclusion reflects scale, relevance to a subsector, public disclosure, or ecosystem importance; it does not assert that each is the best investment or most profitable company.

- **JPMorgan Chase** — diversified banking, cards, commercial banking, payments, investment banking, and asset management. The 2023 annual report documents $158.1 billion revenue, $49.6 billion net income, and $8.3 billion commercial-banking payments revenue.
- **Bank of America** — consumer banking, wealth, commercial banking, investment banking, markets, and payments; 2024 annual report discloses $101.9 billion revenue and $27.1 billion net income.
- **Visa** and **Mastercard** — global payment networks with large transaction volumes and network effects; Visa’s official 2025 report site provides current scale metrics.
- **Fiserv** — merchant acquiring, issuer processing, core banking, and Clover SME software/payments; a relevant incumbent/fintech-infrastructure candidate.
- **PayPal** — digital wallets, checkout, merchant services, and cross-border payments; use its [investor-relations annual-report and SEC-filing hub](https://investor.pypl.com/financials/annual-reports-and-proxy-statements/default.aspx) for current reported metrics.
- **Block** — Square merchant ecosystem and Cash App consumer ecosystem; relevant for integrated SME and consumer fintech, with profitability and regulatory diligence required.
- **Adyen** and **Stripe** — global digital-native merchant acquiring/payment infrastructure. Adyen is public; Stripe is private, so public financial comparability is more limited.
- **Intuit** — small-business financial software, payments, payroll, and consumer tax/credit ecosystem; relevant to embedded finance and distribution through software.
- **Robinhood**, **SoFi**, and **Nubank** — public-market digital finance candidates spanning brokerage, digital banking, lending, payments, and cards; compare unit economics, funding, credit losses, and customer acquisition rather than headline growth alone.
- **BlackRock**, **Charles Schwab**, and **Fidelity** — asset management/brokerage leaders with recurring or transaction-linked fees; market-level and mix sensitivity matter.
- **Progressive**, **Chubb**, and **Lemonade** — insurance and insurtech candidates; evaluate combined ratio/loss ratio, retention, and reinsurance rather than treating all fintech metrics as interchangeable.

## Customer-quality and satisfaction signals

### Measured satisfaction and loyalty

The [J.D. Power 2024 U.S. Retail Banking Satisfaction Study](https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2024-us-retail-banking-satisfaction-study/) surveyed **105,355** retail customers. Overall satisfaction declined one point on a 1,000-point scale while trust fell for a second consecutive year. **13%** said they were likely to switch institutions within 12 months; 8% said they had changed their primary bank, up from 5% in 2018. Among likely switchers, 29% cited too many/high fees and 26% cited a poor service experience. The study identifies trust, people, account offerings, channel choice, saving time/money, digital channels, and problem resolution as its seven dimensions. These are useful customer-quality signals, but the study covers large US retail banks and should not be generalized to global fintechs.

JPMorgan’s 2023 annual report gives company-reported operating signals: more than 24 million multi-line-of-business customers, all-time-high satisfaction across branch and digital channels, a complaint rate per account down nearly 10% year over year, below-historic attrition, and a healthy net-promoter score. These are useful trend indicators but are not independently audited cross-company rankings.

### Complaints and conduct quality

The [CFPB Consumer Complaint Database](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/consumer-complaints/) provides free downloadable data and an API. The CFPB says 98% of complaints sent to companies receive timely responses, but explicitly warns that the data are **not a statistical sample**, are not necessarily representative, and must be normalized for company size/market share. Appropriate metrics include complaints per million accounts or customers, response timeliness, company response category, repeat issues, and trend by product—not raw complaint totals.

### Access, resilience, and inclusion

The Federal Reserve’s 2024 survey provides customer-quality and access indicators beyond satisfaction: 6% unbanked, 11% of banked adults paying an overdraft fee in the prior year, 62% very confident a credit-card application would be approved, and material demographic gaps in account ownership and credit outcomes. These signals help evaluate whether a provider’s growth reflects useful access or potentially harmful pricing/servicing outcomes.

## Recommended 20/20 data fields

- Revenue by net interest income, interchange/processing, subscription/software, advisory, asset-management, insurance premium, and other fees.
- Net income, operating margin, return on equity/tangible equity, efficiency ratio, and free cash flow where meaningful.
- Deposits, loans, payment volume, processed transactions, purchase volume, assets under management, active accounts, and funded accounts.
- Net interest margin, cost of funds, charge-offs, delinquency, provision expense, loss ratio, combined ratio, and reserve coverage.
- Customer acquisition cost, active-user growth, retention/churn, product-per-customer, cross-sell, multi-product penetration, and cohort contribution margin.
- Complaint volume normalized by customers/accounts, regulatory actions, fraud loss, chargeback rate, uptime, payment authorization rate, and resolution time.
- Satisfaction/NPS/CSAT, trust, switching intent, digital adoption, and branch/service experience.
- Inclusion metrics: unbanked/underbanked reach, approval/denial rates, affordability, overdraft incidence, and demographic outcome gaps.
- Capital, liquidity, leverage, common-equity ratios, funding concentration, and regulatory-capital headroom.

## Caveats and interpretation rules

- **Do not rank companies from this report.** The candidate list is a diligence universe, not a measured league table.
- Bank profitability is highly rate-cycle- and credit-cycle-dependent. A single strong year can reflect unusually favorable net interest income, credit, or market conditions.
- Payment volume is not revenue, and revenue is not profit. Compare take rate, mix, fraud/chargebacks, operating expense, and capital intensity.
- Fintech labels cover very different businesses. Separate regulated balance sheets, payment networks, merchant processors, SaaS/API infrastructure, brokerages, insurers, and crypto businesses.
- Private-company data are less comparable than SEC/annual-report data. Stripe and Fidelity, for example, do not provide the same public disclosure as listed peers.
- Customer complaints are a signal of reported problems, not a direct estimate of harm or satisfaction. Normalize for scale and pair with survey, retention, and remediation data.
- Several figures above are as-of 2023–2025. Preserve fiscal year, currency, units, and GAAP/non-GAAP definitions when importing into the refinery.
