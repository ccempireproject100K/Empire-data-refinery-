# 20/20 Data Refinery — Sector 14: Media, entertainment and gaming

## Bottom line

**Media, entertainment and gaming is financially attractive in the 2020s because digital distribution scales globally, monetization is increasingly recurring or multi-layered, and successful intellectual property (IP) can be reused across subscriptions, advertising, games, licensing, live events, merchandise and physical experiences.** The sector is not uniformly profitable: hit-driven content, high customer-acquisition costs, rights inflation, platform fees, regulation and subscription fatigue create material dispersion between winners and losers.

The strongest measured evidence in this research is: (1) U.S. video-game activity reached a government-reported **$184 billion global market in 2023**, with **95% of purchases digital** and mobile representing **49%** of global value ([U.S. International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/media-entertainment-video-games-sector)); (2) Netflix reported **2024 revenue of $39.0 billion, up 16%**, **operating income of $10.4 billion**, a **27% operating margin**, and **301.6 million paid memberships**, up 16% ([Netflix 2024 Form 10-K, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1065280/000106528025000044/nflx-20241231.htm)); (3) Electronic Arts reported **$5.55 billion of live-services and other revenue** in fiscal 2024, equal to **73% of total net revenue**, alongside a **77.4% gross margin** ([EA 2024 Form 10-K, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/712515/000071251524000023/ea-20240331.htm)); and (4) ACSI measured U.S. video-streaming satisfaction at a record **79/100 in 2024**, with Amazon Prime Video at **82** ([ACSI Telecommunications Study 2024](https://theacsi.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/24jun-telecom-study-FINAL.pdf)).

These are **sector signals, not a complete ranking**. The data mix global and U.S. measures and different fiscal years; companies are candidates for screening, not an asserted league table.

## Why the sector can be lucrative in the 2020s

### 1. Digital delivery expands reach and lowers marginal distribution cost

Video, music and games can be distributed globally through broadband, app stores, connected TVs and digital storefronts. The ITA reports that **95% of video games purchased in 2023 were digital**; this supports faster release, global reach and post-launch monetization, although platform commissions and marketing costs remain important deductions ([ITA](https://www.trade.gov/media-entertainment-video-games-sector)).

Netflix demonstrates operating leverage at scale: 2024 streaming revenue grew **16%**, while operating income grew **50%** to $10.4 billion and margin increased to **27%** ([Netflix 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1065280/000106528025000044/nflx-20241231.htm)). This is measured company evidence, not proof that every streaming service has the same economics.

### 2. Recurring revenue and layered monetization improve lifetime value

Subscriptions create recurring revenue; advertising-supported tiers add a second monetization path; games can combine full-game sales with downloadable content, subscriptions, advertising and free-to-play purchases. EA reported that live services and other revenue was **$5.547 billion** in fiscal 2024, versus $4.998 billion in fiscal 2022, and described extra content, subscriptions and advertising as part of this category ([EA 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/712515/000071251524000023/ea-20240331.htm)).

The mechanism is attractive when engagement persists: a sports or multiplayer game can earn from the initial purchase and then from content and services over a longer player relationship. However, EA’s filing also shows live-services revenue was only **1% higher year over year** in fiscal 2024, so recurring monetization should not be confused with automatic high growth.

### 3. IP can be reused across multiple profit pools

Franchises and characters can generate film or episodic content, streaming subscriptions, games, consumer products, licensing, attractions and live experiences. Disney’s 2024 filing explicitly describes this multi-segment model: Entertainment generated revenue from networks, direct-to-consumer, content sales/licensing and other; Experiences generated admission, food, hotels, cruises, merchandise and IP royalties ([Disney 2024 Form 10-K, SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1744489/000174448924000276/dis-20240928.htm)).

Disney reported 2024 revenue of **$91.361 billion**: Entertainment $41.186 billion, Sports $17.619 billion and Experiences $34.151 billion. Segment operating income was $3.923 billion, $2.406 billion and $9.272 billion respectively. The Experiences segment is a useful example of a high-value physical extension of media IP; it is not evidence that all content segments have comparable margins.

### 4. Large, engaged audiences attract advertising and commerce

Audience scale supports advertising, sponsorships, commerce, licensing and data-informed personalization. PwC’s public 2026–30 outlook forecasts global entertainment and media revenue reaching **$4.2 trillion by 2030** at a **3.4% CAGR**, with advertising reaching **$1.4 trillion**; it also forecasts OTT revenue at a **6.1% CAGR** to 2030 ([PwC Global Entertainment & Media Outlook 2026–30](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/business-model-reinvention/outlook/insights-and-perspectives.html)). This is a reputable forecast rather than realized historical performance, and it should be treated as directional.

PwC also flags the counterforce: subscription fatigue and limited willingness to pay for many services. It expects OTT advertising to rise from 19.4% of OTT revenue to 22.6% by 2030. This makes ad-supported tiers and bundling strategically relevant, but increases exposure to advertising cycles and privacy/regulatory constraints.

### 5. Live and in-person experiences provide scarcity and pricing power

Concerts, sports, theme parks, cinema, esports events and immersive venues cannot be perfectly copied at home. The U.S. Census Bureau reports that promoters of performing arts, sports and similar events grew from **$26.3 billion to $41.6 billion** in revenue from 2021 to 2022, surpassing 2019 levels; amusement and theme-park revenue reached **$25.8 billion in 2022**, also above 2019 ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2025/04/entertainment-travel-recreation.html)). These figures document post-pandemic recovery and demand, not a forecast of future profitability.

## Most relevant industries / subsectors

1. **Streaming video and connected-TV media:** subscription video-on-demand, ad-supported streaming, FAST channels, live sports and TV bundles.
2. **Film, television and premium content production/distribution:** studios, theatrical distribution, licensing, production services and content libraries.
3. **Digital music and audio:** streaming music, podcasts, rights, creator tools and live-music discovery.
4. **Video games:** console, PC, mobile, free-to-play, premium, downloadable content, live services and game engines/publishing.
5. **Interactive entertainment platforms:** app stores, digital storefronts, creator platforms, user-generated content, esports and social gaming.
6. **Sports media and betting-adjacent entertainment:** rights, sports networks, streaming rights, sponsorships and fan commerce. Betting is highly regulated and should be screened separately.
7. **Live entertainment:** concerts, festivals, theater, promoters, ticketing and venues.
8. **Theme parks, attractions and immersive experiences:** admissions, hotels, cruises, food, merchandise, licensing and branded attractions.
9. **Advertising technology and media sales:** digital video, connected-TV advertising, programmatic inventory and measurement.
10. **Publishing and creator economy:** books, comics, newsletters, short-form video, influencers and fan monetization.

## Candidate leading companies for a 20/20 screening universe

These are **candidate leaders or strategically important public companies**, selected by scale, IP, distribution, platform position or recurring monetization. They are not ranked because the available sources do not provide a single comparable league table.

- **Streaming / filmed entertainment:** Netflix, The Walt Disney Company, Warner Bros. Discovery, Comcast/NBCUniversal, Paramount Global, Amazon, Apple and Sony.
- **Music / audio:** Spotify, Universal Music Group, Warner Music Group and SiriusXM.
- **Games / interactive entertainment:** Microsoft/Xbox, Sony/PlayStation, Nintendo, Electronic Arts, Take-Two Interactive, Activision Blizzard (now part of Microsoft), Tencent, NetEase, Roblox, Epic Games and Valve.
- **Platforms / distribution / advertising:** Alphabet/YouTube, Meta, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance/TikTok, Roku and Spotify.
- **Live / physical experiences:** Live Nation Entertainment, Sphere Entertainment, Disney, Comcast/Universal and Merlin Entertainments.

The best directly measured examples in the sources are **Netflix, Disney and Electronic Arts**. Netflix supplies streaming scale and margin evidence; Disney supplies cross-segment IP and experience economics; EA supplies live-service and digital-game monetization evidence.

## Customer-quality and satisfaction signals

### Public measured signals

- **Video streaming:** ACSI’s 2024 survey, conducted over the 12 months ending March 2024, scored the category **79/100**, up 3% year over year. Amazon Prime Video led at **82**; Peacock scored 80; YouTube Premium 80; Apple TV+ 79; Hulu 79; Netflix 79; Disney+ 77. ACSI also notes that complaint handling fell 6% to 68, so headline satisfaction should be paired with service-recovery data ([ACSI 2024 study](https://theacsi.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/24jun-telecom-study-FINAL.pdf)).
- **Engagement and adoption:** ITA reports more than **190 million Americans** play games, 78% of U.S. households played on at least one gaming device in the prior 12 months, and there were more than **3.2 billion gamers worldwide** in 2023 ([ITA](https://www.trade.gov/media-entertainment-video-games-sector)). Reach and participation are customer-demand signals, not satisfaction scores.
- **Game-related economic and participation data:** The Entertainment Software Association reports that its 2024 economic-impact report found **$101 billion** in U.S. annual economic output and nearly **350,000 U.S. jobs**, while its public data hub reports **$60.8 billion** in U.S. consumer video-game spending in 2025 ([ESA data hub](https://www.theesa.com/data-insights/)). These are industry-association sources and should be cross-checked for methodology and year before comparisons.
- **Company engagement metrics:** Netflix’s 301.6 million paid memberships and EA’s live-services revenue are useful scale/monetization indicators. They do not by themselves establish satisfaction; pair them with retention, churn, usage, complaints, app ratings and independent surveys.

### Recommended customer-quality fields for the 20/20 data refinery

- Net adds, paid subscribers, monthly active users, daily active users and hours watched/played.
- Gross and net churn, retention cohorts, renewal rates, average revenue per user and customer-acquisition cost.
- ACSI or other independent satisfaction score, NPS where methodologically disclosed, app-store ratings, review volume and complaint rates.
- Content completion, repeat viewing, session frequency, player retention, engagement by cohort and live-event attendance.
- Refund rates, moderation/safety incidents, outage minutes, crash rates and complaint-resolution time.
- Digital share of sales, recurring/live-services share, ad load, ad fill and advertising yield.
- Per-user spending, attach rate for add-on content, conversion from free-to-play to payer and concentration of revenue in top franchises.

## Financial screens and risks

**Potentially attractive screens:** high recurring-revenue mix; strong IP with several monetization windows; rising digital share; positive operating cash flow; durable engagement; low churn; pricing power; and a customer-quality score that is stable or improving.

**Key risks:** hit-driven volatility; content and sports-rights inflation; high production budgets; platform/store commissions; piracy; audience fragmentation; subscription fatigue; advertising downturns; data/privacy and online-safety regulation; labor disputes; toxic or unsafe community behavior; loot-box and gambling regulation; and rapidly changing technology. Disney’s filing specifically identifies demand, content availability, advertising markets, technology, labor and regulation as factors that can affect profitability ([Disney 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1744489/000174448924000276/dis-20240928.htm)).

## Ranking rationale for 20/20 use

A defensible ranking should **not** simply rank companies by revenue. Use a two-layer approach: (1) rank subsectors by measured structural attractiveness—digital reach, recurring/advertising monetization, engagement, margins and IP reuse; then (2) score companies on normalized financial quality and customer quality. Keep factual rankings separate from analyst judgment, label every metric’s geography and period, and do not compare a forecast CAGR with an audited historical result as if they were equivalent.

## Caveats

- The evidence spans 2022–2026 releases and mixes fiscal years, calendar years, U.S. measures and global estimates.
- PwC’s 2026–30 figures are forecasts; ITA and ESA figures include industry-definition and methodology choices that should be checked before constructing a time series.
- Customer satisfaction is measured for U.S. streaming services by ACSI and should not be generalized to global users or to games without comparable survey coverage.
- Candidate companies are a screening universe, **not an invented ranking**. Profitability and quality vary substantially by business model and year.
