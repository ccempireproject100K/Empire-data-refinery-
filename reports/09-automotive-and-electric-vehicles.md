# Automotive and electric vehicles — 20/20 sector report

## Bottom line

**Sector judgment:** Automotive and electric vehicles is a financially consequential 2020s sector with a strong growth runway, but it is not uniformly profitable. The attractive opportunity is the broader value chain—vehicles, batteries, charging, software/electronics, energy storage, components, and services—rather than assuming every EV maker earns attractive returns. The evidence is strongest for expanding demand, manufacturing scale, recurring infrastructure needs, and improving economics in selected segments. Competitive intensity, price cuts, tariffs, policy dependence, capital intensity, and uneven customer experience remain material constraints.

## Why it can be lucrative/profitable in the 2020s

1. **Large and still-expanding unit market.** The International Energy Agency (IEA) reports that global electric-car sales exceeded **17 million in 2024**, up more than 25% year over year, with EVs above 20% of new-car sales. It expects sales to exceed **20 million in 2025**, about one-quarter of global new-car sales. China sold more than 11 million EVs in 2024; the U.S. sold about 1.6 million. These are measured sales, not a claim that all participants are profitable. [1](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/trends-in-electric-car-markets-2)

2. **Scale and platform economics.** EVs combine a high-value durable product with software, financing, insurance, service, charging, data, and energy-storage adjacencies. Tesla’s 2024 Form 10-K says it produced about 1.773 million and delivered about 1.789 million consumer vehicles, deployed 31.4 GWh of energy-storage products, and offers maintenance, installation, operation, charging, insurance, financial, and other services. Its filing also reports energy-generation-and-storage gross margin rising from 18.9% to 26.2% in 2024. This is company-reported evidence of one profitable adjacent segment, not proof that the whole EV sector has comparable margins. [2](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1318605/000162828025003063/tsla-20241231.htm)

3. **Lower running costs can support adoption and willingness to pay.** The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) summarizes evidence that EV owners can save on fuel and estimates roughly **$8,000–$12,000** in lifetime maintenance savings versus conventional vehicles; it also records median range increasing from 68 miles in 2011 to 234 miles in 2021. These savings improve the consumer value proposition, although realized savings depend on electricity, fuel, utilization, repair, insurance, and purchase-price differentials. [3](https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-12/charging-into-the-future-the-transition-to-electric-vehicles.htm)

4. **Infrastructure is a growing capital and service market.** IEA reports that more than **1.3 million public charging points** were added globally in 2024, taking the stock above 5 million; the U.S. reached just under 200,000 public points and Europe just over 1 million. Fast and ultra-fast charging requires hardware, installation, grid upgrades, software, payments, maintenance, and site operations. This creates revenue pools beyond vehicle sales, though utilization and local regulation determine returns. [4](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-charging)

5. **Battery and manufacturing localization create investable bottlenecks.** IEA estimates 17.3 million electric cars were produced in 2024, with China producing 12.4 million—over 70% of global output. It also reports nearly 785 EV models available in 2024 and expects more than 1,000 by 2026 based on announced launches. Battery cells, materials, power electronics, thermal systems, manufacturing equipment, recycling, and localized assembly are therefore strategically important, but concentration and tariffs also create supply-chain and policy risk. [5](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/trends-in-the-electric-car-industry-3)

6. **Established OEMs demonstrate that automotive can produce large profits, even while EV transitions are costly.** Toyota reported FY2025 sales revenue of ¥48.0 trillion and operating income of ¥4.7955 trillion; Toyota and Lexus sold 10.274 million vehicles, of which 4.732 million (46.1%) were electrified, mostly hybrids. The filing attributes profit improvement to price revisions, incentive control, cost reduction, and value-chain earnings. This is evidence for the profitability of a scaled, multi-powertrain automotive platform—not a ranking of EV specialists. [6](https://global.toyota/pages/global_toyota/ir/financial-results/2025_4q_presentation_2_en.pdf)

7. **Policy and regulation accelerate demand and force product change.** BLS documents U.S. federal charging funding and EV purchase credits, while IEA documents emissions standards, trade measures, and national manufacturing incentives. Such policies can expand the addressable market and support domestic supply chains, but policy reversals can slow demand and compress returns. [3](https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-12/charging-into-the-future-the-transition-to-electric-vehicles.htm) [5](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/trends-in-the-electric-car-industry-3)

## Most relevant industries / value-chain segments

- **Light vehicles:** passenger cars, SUVs, pickups, commercial vans, hybrids, PHEVs, and BEVs. Revenue is large, but manufacturing, warranty, distribution, and inventory are capital-intensive.
- **Batteries and materials:** cells, modules, packs, cathode/anode materials, lithium/nickel/graphite processing, battery-management systems, thermal management, recycling, and second-life storage.
- **Automotive components and electronics:** semiconductors, inverters, electric motors, drivetrains, sensors, ADAS, power electronics, wiring, lightweight materials, and thermal systems.
- **Charging and energy services:** home, workplace, fleet, depot, public, fast and ultra-fast charging; charge-point operators; charging software, payments, roaming, grid services, and installation.
- **Energy storage and distributed energy:** stationary batteries, solar-plus-storage, virtual power plants, and energy-management software. Tesla’s filing illustrates the adjacency and reports 31.4 GWh deployed in 2024. [2](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1318605/000162828025003063/tsla-20241231.htm)
- **Automotive software and digital services:** vehicle operating systems, connected services, fleet telematics, navigation, driver assistance, autonomous-driving development, cybersecurity, subscriptions, and over-the-air updates.
- **Automotive finance, insurance, leasing, maintenance, repair, and used vehicles:** recurring or lifecycle revenue streams with different credit and residual-value risks.
- **Commercial and heavy-duty electrification:** buses, delivery fleets, trucks, charging depots, and route-optimization software; economics depend heavily on utilization and total cost of ownership.

## Candidate leading companies (not a fabricated ranking)

These are **screening candidates**, selected because of scale, technology, reported operating evidence, or strategic position. The sources do not establish a single cross-company ranking.

- **Toyota Motor** — global scale, strong hybrid/electrified volume, and measured FY2025 operating profit; a useful benchmark for profitable incumbent execution. [6](https://global.toyota/pages/global_toyota/ir/financial-results/2025_4q_presentation_2_en.pdf)
- **Tesla** — EV manufacturing scale, charging network, software/ADAS exposure, and energy-storage adjacency; 2024 filing reports 1.789 million deliveries and 26.2% energy gross margin. [2](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1318605/000162828025003063/tsla-20241231.htm)
- **BYD** — candidate for integrated Chinese EV and battery scale; IEA identifies Chinese OEMs as dominant in China’s production and notes China produced 12.4 million EVs in 2024. The IEA data support the market position of Chinese OEMs collectively; they do not by themselves rank BYD versus peers. [5](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/trends-in-the-electric-car-industry-3)
- **Volkswagen Group, Hyundai Motor Group, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, and General Motors** — major incumbent OEM candidates with global brands, manufacturing, financing, and EV model pipelines. Candidate status reflects scale and relevance, not a claim of superior profitability.
- **Ford Motor** — relevant North American incumbent and commercial/fleet player; evaluate Ford Blue, Pro, and Model e separately because segment economics differ. Use the company’s latest annual report and SEC filings for current figures.
- **CATL, LG Energy Solution, Panasonic Energy, and Samsung SDI** — battery-cell candidates with strategic relevance to EV supply; compare disclosed capacity, customer concentration, chemistry, pricing, and margins rather than assuming leadership from name recognition.
- **ChargePoint, EVgo, Electrify America, Tesla Supercharging, Shell Recharge, BP Pulse, and Fastned** — charging-network candidates. IEA’s infrastructure data show market expansion, while utilization, capex per site, power costs, uptime, and policy support determine profitability. [4](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-charging)
- **Aptiv, BorgWarner, Bosch, Denso, Valeo, and Magna** — component/electronics candidates exposed to electrification content, ADAS, power electronics, and contract manufacturing.
- **Albemarle, SQM, Umicore, and recycling specialists** — battery-material candidates; highly exposed to commodity-price cycles, project execution, permitting, chemistry substitution, and Chinese supply-chain concentration.

## Customer-quality and satisfaction signals

The strongest freely available consumer signal located is JD Power’s **2025 U.S. Electric Vehicle Experience (EVX) Ownership Study**, fielded August–December 2024 among 6,164 owners of 2024–25 model-year BEVs and PHEVs. It measures stated first-year ownership satisfaction across range accuracy, public charging availability, battery range, cost of ownership, driving enjoyment, home charging, styling, safety/technology, service, and quality/reliability. [7](https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2025-us-electric-vehicle-experience-evx-ownership-study/)

Measured findings include:

- BEVs were **9.1% of U.S. retail sales in 2024**, up from 8.4% in 2023, according to JD Power’s retail-sales data cited in the study. [7](https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2025-us-electric-vehicle-experience-evx-ownership-study/)
- Overall satisfaction averaged **756** for premium BEVs and **725** for mass-market BEVs. The BMW iX scored 790 and ranked highest overall; the Hyundai IONIQ 6 scored 751 and ranked highest in the mass-market segment. These are study scores, not a universal quality ranking across all vehicles or markets. [7](https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2025-us-electric-vehicle-experience-evx-ownership-study/)
- **94% of BEV owners** said they were likely to consider another BEV for their next vehicle, while only 12% were likely to consider replacing it with an ICE vehicle. This is a useful loyalty/repurchase-intent signal, but it is survey intent rather than observed repurchase behavior. [7](https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2025-us-electric-vehicle-experience-evx-ownership-study/)
- Mass-market BEV satisfaction with public-charger availability rose 86 points year over year to 396, while premium BEV satisfaction on the same factor was 551. The gap shows that charging experience remains a customer-quality bottleneck and a potential differentiator. [7](https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2025-us-electric-vehicle-experience-evx-ownership-study/)
- Seven of the ten BEV models with the fewest reported problems were mass-market models, and JD Power links vehicle quality with overall ownership satisfaction. This supports screening for reliability, service experience, warranty claims, software defects, and charger uptime rather than relying on sales growth alone. [7](https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2025-us-electric-vehicle-experience-evx-ownership-study/)

## Metrics available for the 20/20 data refinery

- Global and regional EV sales, share of new-car sales, fleet stock, and growth rate (IEA).
- EV production by country/OEM, model count, export/import exposure, and announced capacity (IEA and company filings).
- Battery demand, GWh capacity, chemistry mix, raw-material prices, cell costs, localization, and recycling volumes (IEA, government data, company filings).
- Public and private charging points by geography, speed, uptime, utilization, capex, electricity cost, and revenue per charger (IEA, AFDC, operator filings).
- OEM revenue, gross margin, operating income, free cash flow, deliveries, average selling price, incentives, inventory, warranty expense, and segment margins (annual reports/SEC filings).
- Energy-storage GWh deployed and gross margin; software and services revenue; subscriptions; insurance loss ratio; financing originations and credit losses (company filings).
- Customer satisfaction, quality problems per 100 vehicles, range accuracy, charger availability, service experience, repurchase intent, warranty claims, recalls, and NPS where publicly disclosed (JD Power, NHTSA, company filings).
- Supply-chain concentration, tariffs, subsidies, emissions targets, tax credits, and permitting/plant announcements (IEA, government sources, company filings).

## Caveats and interpretation rules

- **Do not equate growth with profit.** EV sales are growing rapidly, but OEM and charging profitability varies widely by model, geography, utilization, incentives, and accounting treatment.
- **Separate powertrains.** Hybrids, PHEVs, BEVs, and ICE vehicles have different economics; Toyota’s 46.1% electrified share is mostly HEVs, not BEVs. [6](https://global.toyota/pages/global_toyota/ir/financial-results/2025_4q_presentation_2_en.pdf)
- **Policy is endogenous risk.** Tax credits, emissions rules, charging grants, and tariffs can materially alter demand, local content, and margins. [3](https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-12/charging-into-the-future-the-transition-to-electric-vehicles.htm) [5](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/trends-in-the-electric-car-industry-3)
- **Customer studies are not audited financial data.** JD Power results are survey-based, U.S.-focused, and model/segment specific. Treat rankings as measured within that study, not as universal rankings. [7](https://www.jdpower.com/business/press-releases/2025-us-electric-vehicle-experience-evx-ownership-study/)
- **Company disclosures are not perfectly comparable.** Fiscal years, segment definitions, currencies, subsidies, joint ventures, and non-GAAP measures differ. Normalize before comparing.
- **Supply-chain concentration and trade barriers matter.** China accounted for over 70% of global EV production in 2024, so tariffs, export controls, local-content rules, and geopolitical disruption can change winners and losers. [5](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/trends-in-the-electric-car-industry-3)
- **Candidate companies are not recommendations.** The list is a diligence universe, not a ranking, forecast, or investment advice.
